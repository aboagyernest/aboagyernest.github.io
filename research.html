Research

My research asks who bears the largest insured losses and how risk transfer should be designed when the tail of the loss distribution is changing. It draws on insurance economics, actuarial science and the statistics of extremes.

Featured Paper

Is the Past a Reliable Guide to the Flood Tail? Nonstationary Event Losses and the Design of the NFIP's Reinsurance Layer

Sole-authored. Under review.

Abstract

The National Flood Insurance Program buys an excess-of-loss reinsurance layer each January; in 2025 it paid $139.9m for cover on event losses of $7–11bn. Using event losses rebuilt from 2.7 million NFIP claims (1978–2026), I fit stationary and nonstationary peaks-over-threshold models and derive the layer that buys the largest reduction in CVaR for the same premium. Under a stationary tail, FEMA's $7bn attachment is close to optimal. The largest events, however, show a severity trend that survives scaling by policies in force; under that trend the optimal attachment rises to $12.6–13.5bn, and a layer attaching at $11.3–11.7bn performs well under both readings.
